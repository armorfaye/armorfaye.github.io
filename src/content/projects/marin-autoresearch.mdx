## The setup

[Marin](https://marin.community)’s **Belay** process is an open queue for improving
Marin’s model. You post an idea, Belay trains it on 8×H100 against the current
recipe at a small width (d512), and judges it at **equal wall-clock time**: if
your change makes each step slower, it gets fewer tokens. Ideas that win then go
up a ladder of wider, deeper, longer-trained models.

My loop sat in front of that queue. Agents read papers, past Belay results, and
the training dynamics of our own runs. They wrote each idea up as a short card
with a mechanism and a falsifier, implemented it behind a flag, and screened it
on a TPU. The ones that looked like they would survive the H100 test got posted.
Every Belay result came back into the loop to recalibrate the screen.

I expected the hard part to be generating good ideas. It wasn’t. The hard part
was knowing whether a win meant anything.

## Where it worked: the optimizer

The cleanest success was in the optimizer. One line of work started from a
simple observation in our own runs: under Muon, the weight matrices “want” to
grow early in training, but the recipe pins them at their initial size. Letting
the radius move, with a decay that pulls it back (MuonC), and giving the
router, head, and embeddings their own decoupled decay (AdamC), produced a series
of wins on Belay’s H100 speedrun. Each follow-up improved on the last.

What made this different from the architecture wins was how the gain behaved
over time. The optimizer changes built their lead early and *kept* it through
the end of training, and when we trained longer the gain held or grew. An
optimizer change acts on every matrix in the model at every step. It doesn’t
supply anything a bigger model would learn on its own, so there is no reason for
scale to make it unnecessary.

The first version of MuonC actually lost on H100. The diagnosis was useful: the
weights grew to about three times the control’s size, which quietly cut the
effective learning rate. The model led for most of the run and then lost in the
anneal. Fixing the equilibrium, rather than tuning a number, is what turned it
into a win.

## Where it didn’t: architecture up the ladder

The architecture side found real speedrun wins too: character n-gram rows folded
into the input embedding, the output head’s rows reused as value embeddings,
an exact-match copy pointer on the output. All of them won at d512 on H100.

Then they went up the ladder, and almost all of them faded. Training the same
d512 model for longer shrank every gain. Going wider was mixed. At the largest
rung, nearly everything was noise.

Looking across the registry, the pattern is consistent. Most of our wins were
**crutches**: they handed the model something a larger or longer-trained model
learns by itself, such as token identity, spelling, or n-gram statistics. A
small model trained briefly benefits a lot from those hints. A big model already
has them. The one class that held up best was computation over the context
(copying from earlier in the document), and even that shrank at every rung.

The uncomfortable conclusion is that **the loop was very good at optimizing
for d512**. That is exactly the wrong target.

## Evaluating end to end: is the change worth it?

An autoresearch loop needs to answer one question: *is this change worth
adding to the model?* A loss delta on one run doesn’t answer it. To answer it
you need the whole chain:

1. a cheap screen that is **calibrated** against the expensive test, not just correlated with it;
2. an equal-time verdict, because a change that costs speed has to pay for it;
3. evidence that the gain survives longer training and larger models;
4. some sense of whether it will still be fast at the real training scale.

The loop started out good at (1) and (2) and blind to (3) and (4). Our own
“does the gain fade within the run” check didn’t help either. Ideas that held
perfectly flat through a single run still vanished at the top of the ladder,
because the fade comes from width, depth, and tokens per parameter, not from time
within one schedule. No single-rung screen can see that.

## Kernels are part of the idea

Early on I pushed the loop away from kernel and systems work, on the grounds
that it was harness tuning, not architecture. That turned out to be half wrong.

The clearest case: the same n-gram feature was implemented two ways. The first
version rebuilt a table every step and lost badly at equal time. The second
folded the same rows into the existing embedding table and became our best
speedrun result. The math was identical, and the implementation decided the
verdict.

So I now think an end-to-end loop has to own its kernels. If it doesn’t, it
should at least accept roughly a **±10% margin in compute efficiency** when
judging an idea, since a reasonable engineer could recover that much with a
better implementation. Otherwise you throw out good ideas because of a slow
first draft, and you keep cheap tricks that happen to fuse well on today’s
hardware. It also cuts the other way: a TPU often underestimated what an idea
would cost on H100, so pricing has to happen on the hardware that matters.

## Looking for ideas that scale

Once it was clear that the loop was overfitting to d512, the fix had to go into
idea generation, not just into another filter at the end. The approach I moved
to is closer to how people actually find ideas that scale:

- **Diagnose the big model, not the small one.** Belay’s larger rung controls
  are public. Look for things that get *worse* from d512 to d1280: gradients
  that grow late, gates that saturate, routers that become unhealthy, data
  domains that improve least with scale. An idea that fixes a problem growing
  with scale should scale by construction.
- **Avoid places where scale already helps.** If bigger models already learn
  something well, an idea that supplies it is a crutch.
- **Require a scaling argument.** Every card has to say what grows with width,
  depth, or tokens that its mechanism serves. A separate critic agent argues
  that the idea is redundant at the top rung, and cards that lose the argument
  get marked “speedrun only.”
- **Borrow evidence from larger runs.** Prefer mechanisms that have been
  validated across a scale sweep elsewhere.

I also tried building a cheap proxy for scale: shrinking the width, warm-starting
from a longer-trained checkpoint, and using a deeper, narrower twin. The depth
twin failed on the first idea I tried. Sixteen layers on their own did not
reproduce the fade. That was a useful negative result, because it says the top
rung changes in other ways too.

## Robustness

A win you can only reproduce on one seed, one base recipe, and one setting of a
hyperparameter is not a win. Some lessons:

- **Seed noise is about the size of the bar.** Small single-seed deltas are not
  evidence, and a lucky control can make every idea look slightly worse (or
  better) for a whole round.
- **Some ideas survive recipe changes and some don’t.** Embedding tables and
  logit caps kept their gains as the base recipe changed underneath them for
  weeks. Attention temperature, router, and Muon tweaks mostly did not. Those
  tend to be retunings of the current recipe, not new mechanisms.
- **Prefer ideas that don’t need tuning.** For MuonC, adding feedback on the
  norm level removed the need to hand-pick the decay strength. A change that
  works across a range of settings is much more likely to transfer than one
  that wins at a single swept value.

## Dynamics change across scale

The last lesson is that “scale” isn’t one axis. Going from Belay’s d1024 rung
to d1280 changes the depth, quadruples the batch, lowers Adam’s β₂, changes
the learning rate, and doubles the tokens, all at once. A fade there could come
from any of those.

Looking at the rung controls directly made the shift visible. Some symptoms
grow steadily with width. For example, one normalization in the MLP input loses
reach as the model widens, because the optimizer pins a small matrix at its
initial size. Other things improve with scale, like the share of gradient going
to the token embedding, and those are exactly where the crutches lived. Late in
training, the routed experts turn out to be nearly the only weights still
learning. That suggests capacity ideas behave very differently from the token
tricks.

This changes what an idea needs to be. An idea that compensates for the small
recipe, such as an effective learning-rate cut disguised as an architecture
change, disappears once the ladder retunes the learning rate per rung. An idea
that addresses a dynamic that gets worse with scale has a chance.

## Where this leaves me

Agents are already good at the inner loop of architecture research: proposing,
implementing, testing, and reading the results. What they are not good at, unless
you build it in, is caring about the right scale. The optimizer work scaled because
it acted on the whole model and fixed a real mismatch in the training dynamics.
The architecture work mostly didn’t, because the loop rewarded whatever helped a
small model most. The next version of the loop starts from the large model’s
problems and works down, owns its implementations, and treats a d512 win as a
hypothesis, not a result.
